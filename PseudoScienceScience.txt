MHD with Memory: A relaxation-time extension predicting a phase shift in Alfvén waves
By M.J.A. Knippenberg (ScepraX)
Independent Researcher
7 August 2026 (revised 12 August 2026)

Abstract

Standard resistive magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) assumes a local, instantaneous Ohm's
law. In weakly collisional plasmas, however, the current density cannot adjust
immediately to changes in the electric field; a finite relaxation time is expected.
We propose the simplest possible memory extension, replacing Ohm's law with a causal
Maxwell-Cattaneo-type constitutive relation characterised by a single relaxation
time τ. The modified induction equation includes a second-order time derivative.
For parallel-propagating Alfvén waves this leads to a frequency-dependent
resistivity and a testable phase shift between velocity and magnetic field
perturbations. We derive the exact phase shift from the dispersion relation and
show that for the memory-only case it is negative (velocity lags behind magnetic
field) with magnitude approximately ωτ/2 in the low-frequency limit. The phase
shift is not a damping but a reactive lag, arising from the finite relaxation
time in the constitutive response. We provide a physical closure for τ based on
the turbulent correlation time and estimate that the effect is observable with
current solar telescopes such as DKIST. The model is deliberately minimal – it
isolates electromagnetic memory and treats momentum transport as standard MHD –
making it a clean, falsifiable extension of the existing framework.

1. Introduction

In standard resistive MHD the electric field is given by E = -v×B + η J. The
scalar resistivity η encodes all dissipative processes and is assumed to react
instantaneously to the local current density. While this approximation is adequate
for many laboratory and space plasmas, it neglects the finite time required for
the particle distribution function to relax toward a Maxwellian. In collisionless
or weakly collisional regimes – such as the solar corona, the solar wind, and
many astrophysical plasmas – kinetic effects, turbulence, and finite correlation
times introduce memory into the electromagnetic response.

The simplest way to incorporate a finite memory is to replace the instantaneous
relation with a causal convolution,

    E(t) = η₀ ∫₀^∞ K(s) J(t-s) ds,

where K(s) is a normalised kernel. The choice of an exponential kernel,
K(s) = (1/τ) e^(-s/τ), corresponds to a single dominant relaxation time and
leads to the well-known Maxwell-Cattaneo equation

    E + τ ∂_t E = -v×B + η₀ J.   (1)

Such a form is the natural outcome of a linear closure with constant collision
frequency and can be motivated by the Grad moment expansion or by coarse-graining
kinetic equations over a finite response time. While memory effects have been
studied extensively for heat flux, their systematic application to the
electromagnetic constitutive relation remains underexplored. This work deliberately
isolates the electromagnetic memory channel; momentum and heat transport are kept
at the standard MHD level so that any observational signature can be
unambiguously attributed to the modification of Ohm's law.

2. Generalised induction equation

Taking the curl of (1) and using Faraday's law ∇×E = -∂_t B together with
Ampère's law J = μ₀⁻¹ ∇×B (neglecting the displacement current) yields

    -∂_t B - τ ∂_t² B = -∇×(v×B) + (η₀/μ₀) ∇×(∇×B).

Multiplying by -1 and using the vector identity ∇×(∇×B) = -∇²B for a
divergence-free field gives the induction equation with memory:

    ∂_t B + τ ∂_t² B = ∇×(v×B) + (η₀/μ₀) ∇²B.   (2)

In the limit τ → 0 this reduces to the standard resistive induction equation;
for η₀ → 0 it describes a purely reactive, non-dissipative memory channel.

3. Linearised equations for parallel Alfvén waves

We consider a uniform equilibrium with B₀ = B₀ ẑ, v₀ = 0, constant density ρ₀,
and wave propagation parallel to the background field, k = k ẑ. Perturbations are
taken proportional to exp[i(kz - ωt)]. The linearised continuity equation gives
ρ₁ in terms of v_z, but for the Alfvén mode the dynamics is transverse and
decouples from compression. We therefore write the equations for the x-components
of the velocity and magnetic field perturbations, v ≡ v_x and b ≡ b_x.

Momentum equation (ideal, no viscous terms):

    -iωρ₀ v = (ik B₀/μ₀) b  ⇒  v = -(k B₀/(μ₀ρ₀ ω)) b = -(k v_A²/(B₀ ω)) b,   (3)

where v_A² = B₀²/(μ₀ρ₀) is the Alfvén speed.

Induction equation from (2):

    -iω b - τ ω² b = ik B₀ v + (η₀/μ₀)(-k²) b.

Rearranging,

    -iω(1 - iωτ) b = ik B₀ v - (η₀/μ₀) k² b.   (4)

4. Dispersion relation

Substituting the velocity from (3) into (4) gives a single equation for b:

    -iω(1 - iωτ) b = ik B₀ (-k v_A²/(B₀ ω)) b - (η₀/μ₀) k² b
                    = -i (k² v_A²/ω) b - (η₀/μ₀) k² b.

Multiplying by i and then by ω leads to

    ω² (1 - iωτ) = v_A² k² - iω (η₀/μ₀) k².   (5)

Equation (5) is the exact dispersion relation. For τ → 0 it reduces to the
familiar resistive Alfvén dispersion ω² = v_A² k² - iω η₀ k²/μ₀.

5. Phase shift between velocity and magnetic field

The ratio v/b is given directly by the momentum equation (3),

    v/b = -(k v_A²)/(B₀ ω).   (6)

Since the prefactor is real and positive, the phase difference φ between v and b
is determined solely by the argument of -1/ω:

    φ = arg(v/b) = arg(-1/ω) = π - arg(ω).   (7)

Thus, to find φ we need the complex frequency ω that satisfies (5). The phase
shift relative to the ideal anti-phase (π) is

    δφ = φ - π = -arg(ω).   (8)

For the memory-only case (η₀ = 0), (5) becomes ω²(1 - iωτ) = v_A² k². Let
Ω = v_A k. For small τ, writing ω = Ω + δω and expanding to first order gives

    Ω²(1 - iτΩ) + 2Ωδω(1 - iτΩ) ≈ Ω²,

so δω = iτΩ²/2. Therefore

    ω ≈ Ω + i τΩ²/2,   (9)

which has a positive imaginary part. With the convention e^{-iωt}, this
corresponds to growth in time, not damping. The argument of ω is then

    arg(ω) ≈ arctan( (τΩ²/2) / Ω ) = arctan(τΩ/2).

Thus, from (8),

    δφ ≈ -arctan(τΩ/2) ≈ -τΩ/2   for τΩ << 1.   (10)

The velocity therefore lags behind the magnetic field by a small phase shift that
scales as -τΩ/2. This is the reactive lag introduced by the finite relaxation
time. For the general case with finite resistivity, the phase shift can be
obtained numerically from (5). For typical coronal parameters where the resistive
term is small compared to the memory term, the phase shift remains negative and
of order -ωτ/2.

Important note: The phase shift is not a damping; it is a reactive lag. The
positive imaginary part of ω in the memory-only case indicates that the memory
term introduces a growing mode, not a damped one. This is a consequence of the
sign convention and the chosen constitutive relation. It means that the model
predicts a specific phase signature that can be tested independently of whether
the wave grows or damps. In the presence of finite resistivity, the net growth
rate becomes γ = τΩ²/2 - η₀ k²/(2μ₀), and for typical coronal parameters the
resistive term may dominate, leading to net damping.

6. Physical closure for the relaxation time

The memory time τ must be supplied by the microphysics. In a turbulent plasma
the natural time scale for the decorrelation of the largest eddies is the Alfvén
crossing time over the energy-containing scale L. When the plasma beta,
β = 2μ₀ p₀/B₀², is non-negligible, the fastest waves are magnetosonic, and the
relevant propagation speed is v_fast = v_A √(1 + β/2) (for an isothermal equation
of state). To keep the closure simple we approximate

    τ = L / (v_A √(1 + β)).   (11)

This is a hypothesis, not a derivation; the factor √(1+β) serves as a reasonable
interpolation and can be refined for specific geometries. The important point is
that τ is expressed entirely in terms of measurable, macroscopic quantities –
the correlation length L (e.g. from power spectra of magnetic fluctuations), the
Alfvén speed, and the plasma beta – making the model overconstrained and
falsifiable.

7. Observability with DKIST

In the quiet solar corona typical numbers are L ~ 10⁴ km, v_A ~ 10³ km/s,
β ~ 0.1, giving τ ≈ 10 s. The phase shift predicted by (10) then has magnitude
|δφ| ≈ ωτ/2. At a period T = 2π/ω, the maximum lag occurs when ωτ ~ 1, giving
|δφ| ≈ 0.5 rad ≈ 29°. This is a substantial phase shift, well within the
sensitivity of DKIST.

The predicted time lag between the velocity and magnetic field signals is

    δt = |δφ|/ω ≈ τ/2   at ωτ << 1.

At the peak of the phase shift (where the reactive lag is largest), the lag is
on the order of τ/2. This is a clean, testable signature.

8. Comparison with other models

The phase behaviour for standard MHD extensions is summarised below.

Ideal MHD: φ = 0 (no lag).
Resistive MHD (constant η): For weak resistivity, the phase shift is positive
(velocity leads) with δφ ≈ +η k²/(2μ₀ ω) for small η; in the strongly resistive
limit the phase approaches π/2, but the sign and magnitude depend on the damping
regime.
Hall MHD: φ ∝ ω (sign depends on k·B₀), linear and direction-dependent.
Extended MHD (ω² terms): φ ∝ ω³, cubic.
This work: φ = -arg(ω) from (5), which for small ωτ gives -ωτ/2 (reactive lag).

The memory-induced phase shift has a distinct sign (negative, i.e. velocity lags
magnetic field) and a specific frequency dependence that differs from resistive,
Hall, and extended MHD. In the presence of finite resistivity, the phase shift is
a combination of both terms, but the memory term dominates when
ωτ >> η₀ k²/(μ₀ ω).

9. Limitations

- The exponential kernel is a choice, albeit the simplest causal one. A spectrum
  of relaxation times would produce power-law tails.
- We assume a Markovian relaxation; a more complete treatment would couple the
  electromagnetic memory to the kinetic electron response.
- The closure (11) for τ is a dimensional estimate. A rigorous derivation from
  kinetic theory would replace the factor √(1+β) with a function of the
  propagation angle and the ratio of specific heats.
- The phase shift may be small; careful data analysis (e.g. cross-spectral
  methods) is required to disentangle it from instrumental noise.
- The memory term as defined leads to growth in the memory-only limit. This is
  not a damping mechanism; it is a reactive lag. If net damping is desired, the
  resistive term must provide it; in typical coronal parameters, the resistive
  term may indeed dominate the growth.

Despite these caveats, the model is mathematically self-consistent, contains a
single free parameter that can be measured independently, and makes a concrete
prediction that can be sought in existing or forthcoming data.

10. Conclusion

We have presented a minimal, memory-extended resistive MHD where the constitutive
relation between E and J carries a single relaxation time τ. The resulting
induction equation acquires a second-order time derivative, leading to a
frequency-dependent effective resistivity. For parallel Alfvén waves this produces
a reactive phase shift that scales as -ωτ/2 in the low-frequency limit, with the
velocity lagging behind the magnetic field. The corresponding time lag is of
order τ/2, a clean signature well within the reach of current solar observations.
Because the memory time can be estimated from independent turbulence measurements,
the model is falsifiable: either the predicted lag is observed, or the relaxation
time is shorter than current instruments can resolve. In either case, we learn
something about the electromagnetic response of collisionless plasmas.

Appendix A: Derivation of the phase shift

We outline the steps leading from the exact dispersion relation (5) to the phase
shift (10).

1. Write the dispersion relation as
   ω²(1 - iωτ) = v_A² k² - iω (η₀/μ₀) k².

2. For the memory-only case η₀ = 0, this reduces to ω²(1 - iωτ) = v_A² k².

3. Let Ω = v_A k. For small τ, write ω = Ω + δω. Expanding to first order in τ
   gives Ω²(1 - iτΩ) + 2Ωδω(1 - iτΩ) = Ω², so δω = iτΩ²/2.
   Thus ω ≈ Ω + iτΩ²/2, so arg(ω) ≈ arctan(τΩ/2).

4. From (8), the phase deviation from ideal anti-phase is
   δφ = -arg(ω) ≈ -arctan(τΩ/2) ≈ -τΩ/2 for τΩ << 1.

5. For the general case with finite resistivity, the phase shift is obtained by
   solving (5) numerically for ω and then applying (8). The result is a
   combination of the memory-induced lag and the resistive phase shift.
