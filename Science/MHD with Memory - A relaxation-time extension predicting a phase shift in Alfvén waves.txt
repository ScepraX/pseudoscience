MHD with Memory: A relaxation-time extension predicting a phase shift in Alfvén waves
By M.J.A. Knippenberg (ScepraX)
Independent Researcher
7 August 2026 (revised 12 August 2026; revised 1 October 2026)

Abstract

Standard resistive magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) assumes an instantaneous Ohm's law. We
ask what a finite relaxation time τ - a memory - in the electromagnetic response
does to Alfvén waves, and whether its effects are visible in current solar data.
There are two physically consistent places for the memory. Microscopically, a
relaxing current gives the electron-inertia term of the generalised Ohm's law, whose
effect on coronal Alfvén waves is of relative order (k d_e)² ~ 10⁻¹⁶ and therefore
negligible. At the mean-field level, the turbulent electromotive force relaxes
toward its diffusive value over the turbulent correlation time, as in the τ-approach
of mean-field theory. For parallel Alfvén waves this gives frequency-dependent
("non-Fickian") turbulent resistivity and viscosity, η_t/(1 - iωτ) and
ν_t/(1 - iωτ). The waves are always damped. The damping rate grows as ω² at low
frequency and saturates above ω ≈ 1/τ. The velocity and magnetic perturbations
acquire a phase shift δφ = (η_t - ν_t) ω / [2 v_A² (1 + ω²τ²)], with the velocity
lagging when η_t > ν_t, peaking at ωτ = 1. With the closure η_t ≈ ν_t ≈ u²τ/3 and
parameters from recent DKIST/Cryo-NIRSP observations, the peak phase shift is at
most about 0.1° × (1 - Pm_t), and the turbulent damping is weaker than the observed
damping of coronal kink waves by a factor of at least about four. The memory effect
is consistent with present solar data but not detectable in it. An earlier version
of this paper placed the memory on the electric field itself. That form is not
passive (it produces growing waves), and its prediction of a phase shift of tens of
degrees observable with DKIST is withdrawn.

1. Introduction

In standard resistive MHD the electric field is E = -v×B + η J: it responds
instantly to the current. In weakly collisional and turbulent plasmas, such as the
solar corona and the solar wind, the response takes time. The questions are where a
memory can enter consistently, how large its effects are, and whether current solar
observations can see them.

The first version of this paper wrote

    E + τ ∂_t E = -v×B + η₀ J.   (1')

This makes the electric field a delayed response to the current. Two problems
follow. It reverses the physical statement it was meant to express, namely that the
current cannot adjust instantly to the field. And it is not passive: for η₀ = 0 it
produces growing Alfvén waves, Im ω = +τΩ²/2, with energy that no source supplies. A
relaxation must damp, never amplify. This revision replaces (1') with the two forms
that are consistent, works out their consequences for Alfvén waves, and compares
them with recent solar data.

2. Two consistent places for memory

2.1 Microscopic memory: a relaxing current

If the current relaxes toward its Ohmic value, as in the Drude model of conduction,
τ_e ∂_t J + J = σ (E + v×B), then

    E + v×B = η J + η τ_e ∂_t J.   (1)

The memory term is the electron-inertia term of the generalised Ohm's law, since η
τ_e = m_e/(n e²) = μ₀ d_e², where d_e = c/ω_pe is the electron skin depth. At
coronal densities (n ~ 10¹⁵ m⁻³), d_e ≈ 0.2 m. For Alfvén waves with wavelengths of
tens to hundreds of megametres, the correction is of relative order
(k d_e)² ~ 10⁻¹⁶. Microscopic memory is real but irrelevant at observable scales.

2.2 Mean-field memory: a relaxing turbulent EMF

The relaxation time the first version actually used (Section 6) is the turbulent
correlation time, not a collision time. That points to mean-field MHD. When the
fields are split into mean and fluctuating parts, the mean induction equation
contains the turbulent electromotive force ℰ = ⟨u'×b'⟩. In the τ-approach (Blackman
& Field 2002; Brandenburg, Käpylä & Mohammed 2004), ℰ does not respond instantly but
relaxes toward its diffusive value over the correlation time τ:

    τ ∂_t ℰ + ℰ = -η_t μ₀ J,   (2)

and the turbulent stress relaxes in the same way toward its viscous value, with
turbulent viscosity ν_t. Here η_t is the turbulent magnetic diffusivity. Mean
advection (V×B) stays instantaneous; only the turbulent part carries memory, so the
model is frame-consistent and passive. Memory effects of this kind have been
demonstrated in simulations of turbulent transport (Hubbard & Brandenburg 2009). For
ωτ ≪ 1, (2) reduces to ordinary (Fickian) turbulent diffusion; for ωτ ≳ 1 it is
non-Fickian. The rest of the paper uses this model.

3. Linearised equations for parallel Alfvén waves

We take a uniform background field B₀ = B₀ ẑ, density ρ₀, no mean flow, and
propagation along the field, k = k ẑ. Perturbations are proportional to
exp[i(kz - ωt)], and we write v ≡ v_x, b ≡ b_x. In Fourier space, (2) and its
viscous counterpart give effective coefficients

    η̃ = η_t/(1 - iωτ),   ν̃ = ν_t/(1 - iωτ).   (3)

Momentum:

    -iω v = (ik B₀/(μ₀ρ₀)) b - ν̃ k² v.   (4)

Induction:

    -iω b = ik B₀ v - η̃ k² b.   (5)

4. Dispersion relation and damping

Eliminating v gives

    (ω + i ν̃ k²)(ω + i η̃ k²) = k² v_A²,   v_A² = B₀²/(μ₀ρ₀).   (6)

For τ → 0 this is the familiar visco-resistive Alfvén dispersion relation. For weak
diffusion, writing Ω = k v_A (Appendix A),

    ω ≈ Ω + (η_t + ν_t) Ω² (Ωτ - i) / [2 v_A² (1 + Ω²τ²)].   (7)

The damping rate is

    γ = -Im ω = (η_t + ν_t) Ω² / [2 v_A² (1 + Ω²τ²)] > 0.   (8)

The waves are always damped; there is no growing mode. At low frequency (Ωτ ≪ 1),
γ ∝ Ω², the Fickian result. Above Ωτ ≈ 1, γ saturates at (η_t + ν_t)/(2 v_A² τ²):
high-frequency waves are damped less than ordinary turbulent diffusion would
predict. This saturation is the cleanest signature of the memory.

For propagating waves the amplitude damping length is L_D = v_A/γ. Observers often
quote the equilibrium parameter ξ_E = L_D/(v_ph P), the number of wave periods over
which the amplitude falls by a factor e (Verth, Goossens & Terradas 2010). For this
model, with v_ph ≈ v_A,

    ξ_E = v_A² (1 + Ω²τ²) / [π (η_t + ν_t) Ω].   (9)

The damping length scales with period as P² at low frequency and becomes independent
of P at high frequency. Its local slope is d ln L_D / d ln P = 2/(1 + Ω²τ²), which
passes through 1 at Ωτ = 1.

5. Phase shift between velocity and magnetic field

From (4),

    v/b = -(k v_A²/B₀) / (ω + i ν̃ k²).   (10)

For an undamped wave, v and b are in anti-phase. We define the phase shift relative
to anti-phase, δφ = arg(v/b) - π. With the convention exp(-iωt), a delayed copy of a
signal carries the factor exp(+iωΔt), so a positive δφ means that v lags b.

To first order in the diffusivities (Appendix A),

    δφ ≈ (η_t - ν_t) Ω / [2 v_A² (1 + Ω²τ²)].   (11)

Three features follow.

- The phase shift measures the difference between turbulent resistivity and
  viscosity, not their sum. If η_t = ν_t
  (turbulent magnetic Prandtl number Pm_t = ν_t/η_t = 1), v and b stay exactly in
  anti-phase and the wave remains a pure Elsasser mode.
- The velocity lags when η_t > ν_t and leads when ν_t > η_t.
- The memory makes δφ rise linearly with Ω, peak at Ωτ = 1, and fall at higher
  frequency. The peak value is

    δφ_max = (η_t - ν_t) / (4 v_A² τ).   (12)

A general point also follows from (10). In any model whose momentum equation is
ideal (ν̃ = 0), δφ = -arg ω exactly, which to first order equals γ/Ω. The phase
shift is then fixed entirely by the damping rate. It cannot be a reactive lag that
is independent of damping, as the first version of this paper claimed.

6. Closure

As in the first version, the correlation time is set by the decorrelation of the
energy-containing eddies: τ ≈ L/v_A for low plasma β, with v_A replaced by the fast
speed v_A √(1 + β/2) at finite β (isothermal). For the turbulent coefficients we use
the standard estimate η_t ≈ u²τ/3, where u is the rms turbulent velocity (Krause &
Rädler 1980), with ν_t of the same order; simulations find turbulent magnetic
Prandtl numbers of order unity (Yousef, Brandenburg & Rüdiger 2003). Then

    δφ_max ≈ (1 - Pm_t) (u/v_A)² / 12,   (13)

    ξ_E,min ≈ (3/π) (v_A/u)²   (at Ωτ = 1, with Pm_t = 1).   (14)

The model has two measurable inputs: the correlation length L, which sets τ, and the
turbulent velocity u, which sets η_t and ν_t.

7. Comparison with solar data

7.1 Coronal parameters

DKIST/Cryo-NIRSP observations of the off-limb corona at 1 s cadence show Alfvénic
Doppler-velocity fluctuations with typical amplitudes of 15-30 km/s at 0.1 R☉ above
the limb, in a medium with Alfvén speeds of 200-1000 km/s in the quiet Sun and
coronal holes. The Doppler-velocity power spectrum follows a power law (≈ f^-1.58)
that extends to about 60 mHz (Morton et al. 2025). Taking L ~ 10⁴ km gives τ ≈ 10-50
s and a memory frequency f_τ = 1/(2πτ) ≈ 3-16 mHz, inside the observed band. Two
cases bracket the parameters:

    Case                    v_A         u         τ      f_τ        (u/v_A)²    δφ_max               ξ_E,min
    Typical, high v_A       1000 km/s   15 km/s   10 s   16 mHz     2.3×10⁻⁴    0.001° × (1-Pm_t)    ≈ 4200
    Favourable quiet Sun    200 km/s    30 km/s   50 s   3.2 mHz    2.3×10⁻²    0.11° × (1-Pm_t)     ≈ 42

The Doppler amplitudes include the waves themselves, not only the small-scale
turbulence that sets η_t and ν_t, so using them for u overestimates the effects. The
table gives upper bounds.

7.2 The phase shift is not observable

Even in the favourable case δφ_max ≲ 0.1°, and it vanishes if Pm_t ≈ 1. Measuring it
would also require the magnetic perturbation b on wave timescales. DKIST has
produced the first Zeeman maps of the off-limb coronal field, with line-of-sight
strengths of 10-30 G, but each map took more than an hour to build (Schad et al.
2024). The wave field δb ≈ (u/v_A) B is about 0.15-4.5 G, and following it at a
cadence of seconds to minutes is not yet possible. The first version's claim that a
phase shift of about 0.5 rad would be "well within the sensitivity of DKIST" is
withdrawn.

7.3 The damping is real but subdominant

The observed damping of propagating kink waves, measured with CoMP from the ratio of
outward to inward wave power, corresponds to ξ_E ≈ 2.7 in an active region (Verth,
Goossens & Terradas 2010) and to a median of 11 (mean 24, range 0.9-298) in the
quiescent corona (Morton et al. 2021). The model gives ξ_E ≥ 42 in the favourable
case and ≥ 4200 in the typical case. Turbulent damping with memory therefore
supplies at most about a quarter of the median quiescent damping, and typically well
under one percent. The observed damping length is close to proportional to 1/f, as
resonant absorption predicts (Verth, Goossens & Terradas 2010). In the CoMP band
(below about 8 mHz) the model's local slope ranges from about 2 in the typical case
down through 1 near f_τ ≈ 3 mHz in the favourable case, so the shape of the
frequency dependence alone does not exclude it; its magnitude does. It cannot be the
main damping mechanism, and nothing in present data requires it. Its saturation at
high frequency points in the same direction as DKIST's finding that wave power
persists to tens of mHz, but its magnitude is far too small to matter for that
result.

7.4 In situ data

Parker Solar Probe measures v and b directly and at high cadence. Near the Sun the
fluctuations are highly Alfvénic, with high normalised cross helicity, and the
residual energy is persistently negative, a magnetic excess (Chen et al. 2020). The
model does not produce a magnetic excess at first order, because |v/b| is unchanged
to that order. Near the Sun δb/B ~ 1 (Bale et al. 2019; Kasper et al. 2019), so
(u/v_A)² ~ 1 and (13) would allow a phase shift of a few degrees. But there the
waves and the turbulence occupy the same scales, the scale separation that
mean-field theory assumes fails, and the model does not apply.

8. What would test it

- In simulations: test-field measurements of η_t(ω) and ν_t(ω) in driven MHD
  turbulence with a guide field can measure the memory directly, including the
  turbulent magnetic Prandtl number that controls the sign and size of the phase
  shift (Hubbard & Brandenburg 2009 for the method).
- On the Sun: the outward-to-inward power-ratio method, applied to DKIST's 1 s
  Doppler data up to about 60 mHz in quiet regions with low Alfvén speed, could look
  for a departure from resonant-absorption scaling near f_τ. The expected
  contribution is at most a few tens of percent of the damping and probably much
  less. This would be a hard measurement, not a clean test.

9. Changes from the first version

- Constitutive relation: memory on the electric field (not passive; growing waves)
  is replaced by memory on the turbulent EMF (passive; always damped).
- Sign: the first version stated that the velocity lags. Under its own exp(-iωt)
  convention, its negative δφ meant that the velocity leads. In this version the
  velocity lags when η_t > ν_t.
- Size: the first version gave |δφ| ≈ 0.5 rad at ωτ ≈ 1, using the small-ωτ formula
  outside its range. Its own exact dispersion relation gives about 0.29 rad (16°) at
  ωτ = 1, approaching 30° only as ωτ → ∞, with no maximum. The consistent model
  gives δφ_max ≲ 0.002 rad.
- Peak: the first version's expectation that the effect peaks near ωτ ≈ 1 does hold
  in the consistent model, for the phase shift (12).
- Comparison models: ideal Hall MHD leaves the momentum equation unchanged, so for
  real ω it gives no v-b phase shift. The first version's "φ ∝ ω" for Hall MHD and
  "φ ∝ ω³" for extended MHD were not derived and are removed. Resistive MHD gives
  δφ = +η k²/(2Ω), so the velocity lags.
- Observability: the DKIST claim is withdrawn (Section 7.2).
- References: an unused reference that could not be verified has been removed, and
  the mean-field, simulation and observational literature has been added.

10. Limitations

- A single exponential memory (one τ) is the simplest choice. A spectrum of
  correlation times would change the high-frequency behaviour.
- The τ-approach is a closure, not a derivation. It requires scale separation
  between the waves and the turbulence, which is marginal in the corona and fails
  near the Sun.
- Only parallel propagation in a uniform medium is treated. Coronal kink waves
  travel in structured, inhomogeneous loops where resonant absorption and phase
  mixing dominate.
- The estimates of u and L are order-of-magnitude.

11. Conclusion

A memory in the electromagnetic response can be added to MHD consistently in two
ways. Microscopically it is electron inertia, and negligible for observable waves.
At the mean-field level it is the τ-approach to turbulent transport: Alfvén waves
are always damped, the turbulent damping saturates above ω ≈ 1/τ, and the velocity
and magnetic perturbations acquire a phase shift, set by η_t - ν_t, that peaks at
ωτ = 1. With parameters from the latest DKIST and CoMP observations, the phase shift
is at most about 0.1°, and the turbulent damping is at most a minor part of the
observed wave damping. The model is consistent with current solar data, but current
solar data cannot detect it. The earlier prediction of a large, DKIST-observable
phase shift does not survive.

Appendix A: First-order solution

1. Write (6) as (ω + iν̃k²)(ω + iη̃k²) = Ω², with Ω = k v_A.

2. Put ω = Ω + δω and keep terms of first order in η_t and ν_t: 2Ω δω + iΩ (ν̃ + η̃)
   k² = 0, so δω = -i (ν̃ + η̃) k²/2, with ν̃ and η̃ evaluated at ω = Ω.

3. Use 1/(1 - iΩτ) = (1 + iΩτ)/(1 + Ω²τ²). Then δω = (η_t + ν_t) k²
   (Ωτ - i) / [2 (1 + Ω²τ²)], which is (7) with k² = Ω²/v_A².

4. For the phase, the imaginary part of ω + iν̃k² is -(η_t + ν_t)
   k²/[2 (1 + Ω²τ²)] + ν_t k²/(1 + Ω²τ²) = (ν_t - η_t) k²/[2 (1 + Ω²τ²)]. Its
   argument is therefore (ν_t - η_t) k²/[2Ω (1 + Ω²τ²)], and from (10),
   δφ = -arg(ω + iν̃k²), which gives (11).

Disclaimer: This work is a self-consistent formal model, not a derivation from
first-principles kinetic theory. Its value lies in stating clearly what a memory in
the electromagnetic response would do, how large it would be on the Sun, and why
current observations cannot yet see it.
